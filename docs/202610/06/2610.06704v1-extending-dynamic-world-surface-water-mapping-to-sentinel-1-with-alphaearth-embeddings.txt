# Extending Dynamic World Surface Water Mapping to Sentinel-1 with AlphaEarth Embeddings 

Rohit Mukherjee, Frederick Policelli, Beth Tellman, TC Chakraborty, Jonathan Giezendanner, Jonathan A. Sullivan, and Ning Sun 

Abstract —Dynamic World (DW) maps land use and land cover globally at 10 m from Sentinel-2 (S2) imagery, but only for cloud-free observations, which limits where and when surface water can be mapped. We use the DW water class as weak supervision for a Sentinel-1 (S1) synthetic aperture radar (SAR) model so that DW-like water maps can be produced for every S1 acquisition. Google’s AlphaEarth Foundations (AEF) annual embedding supplies spatial context, while S1 backscatter supplies the acquisition-time observation. On 53 globally distributed scenes with independent annotations of 3 m PlanetScope imagery acquired within 48 h of the S1 overpass, the S1-only model already reaches a pooled water intersection over union (IoU) of 0.77, comparable to 0.75 for the operational OPERA DSWx-S1 product, and adding AEF raises it to 0.85. The fused model improves on the S1-only model on 44 of 53 scenes and exceeds OPERA on 48, and on the independent S1S2-Water benchmark it reaches 0.94, compared with 0.87 for OPERA. Optical land-cover products can thus provide scalable training labels for SAR surface water mapping. 

Index Terms —AlphaEarth, Dynamic World, foundation models, sensor fusion, Sentinel-1, surface water, synthetic aperture radar (SAR), weak supervision.

OPERA DSWx-S1, for example, uses land cover, long-term water occurrence, and height above nearest drainage (HAND) to reduce such false detections [11]. We ask whether a model can reproduce DW water maps from S1 on cloudy days, complementing S2-based DW to increase observation frequency. To reduce false detections over radar-dark land, we add Google’s AlphaEarth Foundations (AEF) embeddings as spatial context. AEF, one of several recent pixel-level embedding products [12], compresses a year of optical, radar, lidar, and other Earth observations into a 64-dimensional embedding for each 10 m pixel [13], encoding persistent land cover and, indirectly, water occurrence. Unlike rule-based masks, AEF lets the model learn which spatial context helps interpret SAR observations. Optical products have supplied weak supervision for water mapping. Sen1Floods11 used S2 spectral-index labels for S1 models [14], while DW labels have been used for fractional water mapping from 375 m VIIRS imagery [15]. We use DW labels for acquisition-specific 10 m SAR water mapping with annual AEF context. Other studies use AEF for U.S. post-event SAR flood segmentation [16] and benchmark foundation-model backbones for flood mapping [17]. Our focus is global surface water mapping with DW pseudo-labels rather than flood-event annotations. This letter makes three contributions. First, we train an S1 water model on DW pseudo-labels alone, without manual annotation. Second, we isolate the contribution of annual AEF context with an S1-only baseline that shares the training data and schedule. Third, we evaluate both models against two references independent of DW, comprising 53 PlanetScope-based annotated scenes [18] and the S1S2-Water benchmark [19], and compare them with the operational OPERA DSWx-S1 product [11], [20]. We then added 600 tiles centered on lakes, seasonal water, reservoirs, and coastal sites using the same pairing and coverage criteria (Supplementary Section S3). The label band of each paired DW image was converted to a binary mask (class 0, water → 1; all other classes → 0) and aligned pixel for pixel with the S1 tile; remaining no-data pixels were also set to 0. Labels therefore inherit DW classification errors [23], surface water changes between paired acquisitions, and cloud-masking errors. The final dataset has 5278 co-registered S1 / AEF / DW triplets, split at random at the tile level (80/20, seeded) into 4222 training and 1056 validation tiles. For training, we sampled 

512 ×512 -pixel patches from these tiles. DW is used only as the training target; at inference the model receives S1 and AEF. 

B. S1 and Annual AEF Fusion 

We obtained S1 ground range detected (GRD) imagery from the Google Earth Engine [24] COPERNICUS/S1_GRD collec-tion (Interferometric Wide swath, dual VV/VH polarization), which provides calibrated, orthorectified σ0 backscatter in dB. We stacked VV, VH, and the incidence angle relative to the ellipsoid ( angle band). The 64-band annual AEF embedding (v1, int8) was retrieved from the Source Cooperative archive [25] and reprojected to the S1 grid by nearest neighbor; it is constant within a calendar year and may include observations after the S1 acquisition. Each training tile uses the embedding of its acquisition year, except 98 tiles acquired in 2015–2016, before the first AEF year, which use the 2017 layer and therefore include later context. S1 and AEF are fused at the input. A bias-free 1×1

convolution projects the 64 AEF bands to k=16 channels. These channels are concatenated with the three S1 bands to form a 19-channel input to a U-Net [26] with an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-34 encoder [27] (Fig. 1(a)). The model is trained in four fine-tuning stages using a combined cross-entropy and Dice loss and the Ranger optimizer, with early stopping based on validation water IoU within each stage. Input bands are standardized using training-set statistics. Training details are provided in Supplementary Section S3 and the code. 

C. Experimental Design 

We compare S1-only and S1+AEF( t) models with the same training split, architecture, and schedule, differing only in the 3

AEF input; t denotes the S1 acquisition year. Both are trained with seeds 42–44; results use seed 42 unless otherwise stated. Additional analyses vary the projected AEF width and the training-set size (each over three seeds) and the embedding year (at inference and in training) (Section III-C; Supplementary Section S4). 

D. Independent Evaluation and Product Comparisons 

The Global Surface Water Dataset (GSWD) [18] contains 100 manually annotated 3 m PlanetScope-based maps spanning rivers, lakes, urban areas, and diverse biomes. We retained 53 with S1 GRD within 48 h of PlanetScope (median 19.9 h). S1 and AEF were exported at the 10 m training resolution. Inference averages four-flip predictions on 512 ×512 -pixel tiles with 64-pixel overlap; probabilities are bilinearly resampled to the 3 m reference grid before thresholding (Supplementary Section S2). Supplementary Section S5 tests inference on inputs resampled to 3 m. GSWD and SWORD are independent sources, but training and evaluation sites were not geographically separated. Five evaluation scenes lie within 5 km of a training-tile centroid and eight within 11 km. Excluding these eight scenes leaves pooled S1+AEF water IoU essentially unchanged (0.855 for the remaining 45 versus 0.851 for all 53). The primary operating point is a fixed threshold of 0.30, selected from a DW-validation threshold sweep of an earlier model and retained to compare all reported models at the same operating point; it was not selected using GSWD or S1S2-Water reference labels. All methods are scored on the same pixels, those with reference values 0 or 1 and valid predictions from every model run and OPERA, comprising 93.4% of the reference grid; scoring each method on its own valid pixels changes no pooled or per-scene mean IoU by more than 0.01. We report pooled precision, recall, and Dice (F1), and water IoU aggregated in two ways. Pooled metrics aggregate confusion counts across scenes, whereas per-scene IoU gives each scene equal weight. Bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs) quantify uncertainty in the per-scene mean, and paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests evaluate within-scene differences between methods. We compare primarily with OPERA DSWx-S1’s 30 m B02 BWTR layer for the same S1 acquisition. Water includes inundated vegetation; HAND-excluded, layover/shadow, ocean, and no-data pixels are invalid. For overlapping OPERA product tiles (28 of 53 scenes), we union valid-pixel and water masks after nearest-neighbor resampling to 3 m. Published OPERA validation scores only entirely water or non-water 30 m pixels [11]; we include all valid pixels, treating boundaries more strictly. Matched 30 m scoring and pure-reference blocks test resolution and boundary sensitivity (Supplementary Section S5). DW is assessed separately because no GSWD scene has a cloud-free DW map within ±4 days of PlanetScope (Section III-D).

A. An S1-Only Model Trained on DW Labels 

Trained solely on DW water labels, S1-only reaches pooled IoU 0.768 on the independent PlanetScope reference, slightly above OPERA (0.747), and exceeds it on 36/53 scenes (Wilcoxon p < 0.05 ). Agreement with DW-independent labels supports optical supervision of SAR water mapping. 

B. Annual AEF Context Improves the SAR Extension 

AEF raises pooled water IoU from 0.768 to 0.851 (Table I), a gain of +0 .083 with the same training split and schedule. Recall rises from 0.854 to 0.950; precision changes little (0.883 to 0.891). Fusion improves on 44/53 scenes (Wilcoxon p < 0.05 ), raising mean IoU from 0.62 (bootstrap 95% CI 0.53–0.70) to 0.74 (0.67–0.81) (Fig. 2). Gains are uneven, with a median of 

+0 .03 , and 13 scenes with gains above 0.2 account for 85% of the summed gain; in six, S1-only misses most water (IoU below 0.2). In WorldCover [28] overlays, 77% of the false negatives corrected by AEF lie in WorldCover permanent water bodies, and 59% of the corrected false positives lie on bare or sparsely vegetated land. AEF also adds false positives, 62% of which lie in WorldCover permanent water bodies that the GSWD reference marks as dry, consistent with AEF encoding persistent water occurrence (Supplementary Section S4).

D. Comparison with Existing Products 

Against OPERA DSWx-S1, the fused model raises pooled IoU by 0.104 and improves on 48/53 scenes (median +0 .10 ,Wilcoxon p < 0.05 ), with higher precision and recall (Table I). The advantage persists with matched resolution. Area-averaging probabilities and scoring both methods at 30 m gives pooled GSWD IoU 0.855 for S1+AEF versus 0.749 for OPERA, with fusion exceeding OPERA on 47/53 scenes. On entirely water or non-water reference blocks, IoU is 0.916 versus 0.825. Across scoring variants, the IoU difference between fusion and OPERA changes by at most 0.013 (Supplementary Table S2). DW requires a nearby cloud-free observation. No GSWD scene has one within ±4 days of its reference, only two have one within ±8 days, and the median offset is 16 days; unlike training sites, GSWD scenes were not screened for DW coverage. DW pseudo-labels provide scalable optical supervision for S1 water mapping. Trained on them alone, the S1-only model matches or slightly exceeds OPERA DSWx-S1 on GSWD (0.77 versus 0.75), and annual AEF context improves accuracy on both GSWD and S1S2-Water. S1 supplies the acquisition-time observation; AEF is fixed within a calendar year and cannot by itself represent within-year changes in water extent. Its role is to supply spatial context for interpreting that observation. The two benchmarks highlight different S1-only errors, and AEF reduces both. On GSWD, AEF mainly recovers missed water, most of it in WorldCover permanent water bodies (Section III-B); these overlays show where errors change, not why. On S1S2-Water, whose scenes cover whole landscapes with little water, S1-only errors are mainly false positives, and AEF raises precision from 0.81 to 0.95. The gain is concentrated in scenes where S1 alone fails. In these experiments, annual context also makes the model less sensitive to training and input choices. Across three training seeds, per-scene IoU varies less for the fused model than for S1-only (SD 0.001 versus 0.007, Table II). Using the S1 images distributed with S1S2-Water instead of our Earth Engine S1 lowers pooled S1-only IoU from 0.82 to 0.72 but fused IoU only from 0.94 to 0.93, and running the network on GSWD inputs resampled to 3 m instead of the 10 m training grid lowers S1-only IoU from 0.77 to 0.57, while the fused model stays at 0.85 5

(seed 42; Supplementary Section S5). S1 preprocessing differs between processing chains, so this stability is useful in practice. The fused model was neither trained nor tuned on S1S2-Water, yet at native resolution (threshold 0.50, as in [19]) its mean per-scene IoU (0.88) is close to the 0.85 reported for the best S1 model trained on that benchmark (Supplementary Section S5). Scene sets and averaging differ, so these results indicate transfer without retraining. OPERA DSWx-S1 reduces errors over radar-dark land with fixed rules on static layers, including WorldCover, JRC water occurrence, and HAND [7], [11]; AEF serves a similar purpose but is learned jointly with S1 from the training labels. HAND could complement our model as an input, while learned embeddings could inform rule-based masks. Several limitations remain. Annual context can favor areas of persistent water, where most false positives added by AEF lie (Section III-B). The model was not designed or evaluated for post-flood inundation. The model can also reproduce systematic DW label errors. The split is tile-level rather than basin-held-out, most training tiles (4678 of 5278) are centered on river reaches, and 10 m predictions have limited accuracy on narrow channels (Supplementary Section S5). Acquisition-year AEF can include observations after the S1 acquisition, so results with acquisition-year AEF are retrospective, not achievable in near real time. Prior-year AEF provides an alternative: using that layer, at inference or in training, retained 94–96% of the pooled IoU gain (Section III-C). The approach still depends on continued AEF availability. The pseudo-label dataset can expand as DW observations accumulate, and the same weak-supervision strategy could be tested with other SAR sensors. Together with optical observations, acquisition-specific S1 maps could support more frequent monitoring of river width, lake and reservoir area, and shorelines. DATA AND CODE AVAILABILITY

SUPPLEMENTARY MATERIAL 

S1. S1S2-W ATER BENCHMARK 

We use the Sentinel-1 reference masks for 15 of the 16 test scenes in S1S2-Water v1.0.1 [19], [29]. Scene 31 was excluded before scoring because its source S1 acquisition could not be reproduced from its metadata and image encoding. The masks were initialized from the S2 normalized difference water index (NDWI) and manually corrected against each sensor’s image. S1S2-Water was assembled for general surface-water mapping rather than flood-event evaluation. “Flooded” in Table S1 denotes a land-cover class, not a documented flood event. No benchmark data were used for training or threshold selection. 

Inputs and scoring. Earth Engine S1 GRD VV, VH, and incidence angle were exported for the 22 source products underlying the 15 evaluated scenes, mosaicked in the order listed in the benchmark metadata, and paired with acquisition-year AEF on a 10 m grid. All three seeds use four-flip augmentation. Scoring uses a 30 m grid. Reference pixels are valid when at least 50% of their area is valid, and water when at least 50% of that valid area is water. Model probabilities are resampled bilinearly before thresholding; OPERA uses nearest neighbor. Common valid pixels cover 92.6–100% of the reference-valid area per scene. 

OPERA processing. Matching SLC acquisitions were processed with RTC-S1 v1.0.4 and DSWx-SAR v1.2, using Copernicus GLO-30 elevation, GLO-30 HAND, WorldCover 2020, and JRC water occurrence. Missing offshore eleva-tion/HAND tiles were filled with zeros. We applied an upstream correction to the DSWx-SAR v1.2 edge-water exclusion to all scenes. B02 BWTR class 1 is water; HAND exclusion, layover/shadow, ocean, and no-data classes are invalid. 

Results. Table S1 uses the fixed 0.30 threshold of the main letter. Fig. S1 and the values below use threshold 0.50, selected on DW validation. At 0.50, fused pooled IoU is 0.953 ±

0.001 versus 0.822 ± 0.024 for S1-only and 0.869 for OPERA. Fused predictions exceed OPERA on 13–14 scenes across seeds (Wilcoxon p ≤ 0.001 ). S2. P LANET SCOPE REFERENCE EVALUATION 

The 53 GSWD reference scenes are 1024 ×1024 pixels at 3 m. Reference values 0 and 1 denote non-water and water, respec-tively, and are valid for scoring; any other value is excluded. S1– PlanetScope offsets have median 19.9 h, maximum 44 h, and are at most 24 h for 30 scenes. Inference uses 512 × 512 tiles with 64-pixel overlap and four-flip augmentation; probabilities are resampled bilinearly to the reference grid before thresholding. B02 BWTR class 1 includes open water and inundated vegetation; classes 250, 251, 254, and 255 are invalid. After nearest-neighbor resampling, overlapping products are com-bined by a union of valid pixels and a union of water (28 scenes, up to eight products). At threshold 0.50, pooled IoU is 0.86 for S1+AEF( t), 0.85 for the prior-year substitution, and 0.77 for S1-only. Figure 2 in the main paper uses 10,000 scene-bootstrap replicates (seed 42) for mean-IoU confidence intervals; paired tests use Wilcoxon signed ranks. S3. T RAINING DATA AND CONFIGURATION 

Sampling. Additional sampling sites came from the SWOT Prior Lake Database [30], JRC Global Surface Water [7], Global Dam Watch [31], and GSHHG [32]. River tiles span October 2015–November 2025, with 95% in 2020–2022; other tiles span 2020–2023. The tile-level split uses seed 42. 

Model and optimization. The four fine-tuning stages comprise up to 2 frozen-encoder, 2 partially unfrozen, 10 fully unfrozen, and 10 tail epochs, with one-cycle learning rates of 

10 −4, 5 × 10 −7–10 −5, 6 × 10 −8–3 × 10 −6, and 1.5 × 10 −7–

1.5 × 10 −6, respectively. The code provides the full training configuration. 

Computing and access. Models were trained on NVIDIA A100 GPUs at the National Energy Research Scientific Computing Center (NERSC). Code, dependencies, and us-age examples are provided at https://github.com/Rohit18/ surfaceWaterMappingGlobal (v1.0). S4. M ODEL SENSITIVITY AND ERROR PATTERNS 

Embedding year. Replacing acquisition-year AEF with prior-year AEF at inference, with the S1+AEF weights fixed (seed 42, threshold 0.30), gives pooled IoU 0.846 versus 0.851 and per-scene mean 0.732 [95% CI 0.662, 0.797] versus 0.741 [0.669, 0.806], retaining 94% of the pooled gain over S1-only (0.768). The per-scene decrease has mean 0.009 and median 0.004 ( p = 0 .004 ); the substituted model exceeds S1-only on 41/53 scenes ( p < 0.01 ). We also trained and applied the S1+AEF model ( k=16 ,seed 42, same schedule) with prior-year AEF. Its training set omits the 137 tiles acquired in 2015–2017, which have no prior-year layer, and one tile with less than 90% prior-year coverage (5140 of 5278 tiles). It reaches pooled IoU 0.848 and per-scene mean 0.734 [0.663, 0.800], retaining 96% of the pooled gain. It exceeds S1-only on 41/53 scenes and OPERA on 48/53 (both p < 0.05 ) and does not differ significantly from the acquisition-year model (mean per-scene difference 0.007, 

p = 0 .07 ). 

Error patterns. Overlaid on WorldCover 2021 [28], using pixels valid in both models, the false negatives corrected by AEF (1.38 M pixels) lie mainly in permanent water bodies (77%) and mangroves (13%); the corrected false positives (0.84 M) lie mainly on bare or sparse vegetation (59%) and tree cover (20%); and 62% of the added false positives (0.81 M) lie in permanent water bodies. AEF raises scene IoU by more than 0.05 on 24 scenes, changes it by at most 0.05 on 26, and lowers it by more than 0.05 on three. These associations describe where errors occur, not their cause. S5.
