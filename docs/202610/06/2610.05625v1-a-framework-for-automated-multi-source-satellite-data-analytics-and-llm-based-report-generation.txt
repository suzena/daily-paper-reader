Abstract —This paper presents the workflow for building an automated ArcGIS Pro tool using ArcPy to extract the Land Surface Temperature (LST) from Landsat 7, 8 and 9 datasets. The tool eliminates the need for manual band selection and repet-itive raster computations by automating the multi-step workflow of radiometric calibration, NDVI-based emissivity correction, and thermal conversion. In addition to supporting batch and single-scene processing, the tool has an optional Large Language Model (LLM) for statistical result interpretation and reporting. Depending on batch size, the tool reduced the processing time from around 11–58 minutes when done manually to around 4–11 minutes using the tool. We tested the tool with data from Ras Al Khaimah (RAK) in the UAE, and the LST obtained for Ras Al Khaimah ranged from approximately 25°C to 50°C, demonstrating an accurate LST mapping compatible with the weather conditions of RAK. In summary, our tool reduces human errors and improves processing accuracy and efficiency for thermal and environmental remote sensing applications, in addition to providing an interactive LLM-based interface for result’s interpretation. 

Index Terms —ArcGIS, Explainable AI (XAI), Remote Sensing, Land Surface Temperature (LST), Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs), Large Geospatial Models (LGMs) 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Land Surface Temperature (LST) is one of the most sig-nificant factors in investigating climate change, urban climate analysis, vegetation assessment, and a wide range of envi-ronmental studies. LST is a measurement of Earth’s surface temperature. It is calculated from satellite imagery and used in a variety of scientific fields, including agriculture, hydrol-ogy, ecology, and meteorology. LST can help in identifying environmental issues, evaluate the severity of droughts, and measure the intensity of heat by using various analytical methods [1], [2]. Several studies on thermal analysis have been conducted using MODIS, ASETR, Landsat Thematic Mapper (TM)/Enhanced Thematic Mapper (ETM), and Landsat 8 data [3], [4]. Typically, a raster calculator is used to o obtain LST from a Landsat imagery in ArcGIS Pro. It performs a sequence of corrections to the thermal band, that mainly consists of the following: conversion of Top of Atmosphere (TOA) Radiance, conversion to Top of Atmosphere (TOA) brightness tempera-tures, and conversion from At-Satellite Temperature to Land Surface Temperature. These steps usually require repeated use of the raster calculator, which increases the risk of user error in addition to increase analysis processing time. Few satellites provide calibrated LST, such as Sentinel 3 and MODIS, however, they have lower resolutions (capped at approximately 1km). This low resolution is typically insufficient for research that requires more precise spatial information in the form of a higher resolution. Consequently, developers and researchers frequently rely on a higher resolution Landsat’s 30m thermal data, which typically imposes a higher processing time. This project addresses this problem by developing an au-tomated LST tool in ArcGIS Pro using ArcPy. The tool accepts Landsat 7, 8, and 9 data files and generate the LST map directly, without running the raster calculator or even opening the data files to extract the required band. The tool can recognize each satellite from the file name and uses the specific constants required for the calculations. We have also integrated a Large Language Models (LLM)-based interface to produce a summarized report based on LST map’s statistics. Our LST tool improves productivity, consistency, and utility for academics and researchers using thermal remote sensing data by automating the entire workflow. The specific objectives of this study are the following: (1) To automate the process of calculating LST and supporting Landsat 7, 8, and 9. (2) to allow single-scene and batch processing. (3) to support LLM-driven reporting and interpretation. (4) To reduce human error in calculating LST and improve the processing accuracy and efficiency. II. L ITERATURE REVIEW 

Land Surface Temperature (LST) has gained widespread recognition as an essential factor in climate and environmental research due to its influence on surface energy exchange and climatic variability. To generate LST values, recent studies utilize Landsat 8 and 9 satellite data. They employ Single-Channel methods on Band 10 or multiple channel Split-Window (SW) algorithms that utilize both Thermal Infrared Sensor (TIRS) Bands 10 and 11 [5]–[7]. The algorithms studied include methods based on the Radiative Transfer Equation (RTE), particularly Single-Channel techniques, and Multi-Channel Split-Window Algorithms (SWA) [5]–[7]. Re-cent studies have shown techniques based on NDVI-derived emissivity values, where they change raw digital numbers to radiance, reflectance, and brightness temperature before synthesizing LST using the Radiative Transfer Equation (RTE) arXiv:2610.05625v1 [cs.AI] 4 Oct 2026 approach with Landsat 8 data [6], [7]. Similarly, recent studies have employed the Split-Window approach using Landsat 8 thermal bands, and they demonstrate that LST estimates differ depending on emissivity and atmospheric correction [6], [7]. Numerous studies have used NDVI-based emissivity models to address emissivity, which remains a major concern in LST extraction. Sekertekin et al. used Surface Radiation Budget Network (SURFRAD) ground measurements for validation to assess many LSE models and retrieval techniques in Landsat 5, 7 and 8 in rural locations [8]. The MWA approach produces the lowest error (RMSE 2.39 K) throughout missions, according to the study’s conclusion that algorithm accuracy differs by sensor type. The study also demonstrates the growing interest in automated, tool-based processes by offering an ArcGIS toolbox that integrates many retrieval techniques. The use of LST analysis in environmental monitoring is the subject of other research. [9] used Landsat-derived trends from 2016 to 2020 and NDVI–LST correlations to evaluate drought severity. The findings confirmed the value of LST as an indicator of agricultural stress, showing astrong correlation (up to 99%) between NDVI and LST . Similarly, [10] used Landsat 8 thermal bands in conjunction with supplementary vegetation indicators to study LST change in agricultural zones. The results demonstrated a significant agreement (RMSE 0.94–1.26°C) between satellite-estimated LST and meteorological station readings. The use of tra-ditional processing procedures, such as determining neces-sary bands, collecting metadata information, and repeatedly performing raster computations, is a common theme in the literature. While a number of studies suggest automated or semi-automatic procedures, the majority are either unable to process batch processing or restricted to single satellite types, or need manual band selection. Furthermore, many procedures still use Level-1 products to adjust intermediate variables such as NDVI, radiance, and emissivity, or to preserve consistency across historical archives. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

In this section, we explain the workflow for constructing an automated tool to calculate the LST, including data prepara-tion, correction factors, and the complete pipeline. 

A. Study Area 

Ras Al Khaimah (RAK) in the UAE, is one of the seven Emirates of the United Arab Emirates (UAE), is the study area that we have chosen for thispaper. It is located at 55° 60 E and 25° 26 N. It borders the Sultanate of Oman and is located in the northernmost region of the United Arab Emirates. It is estimated that RAK has 400,000 residents and a total area of 2478 square kilometers in 2025. 

B. Input Data 

Level-1 Landsat images from Landsat 7, Landsat 8, or Landsat 9 can be used as an input for our tool. Each scene input to the tool must use the file name generated by Earth Explorer, and the file must contain the thermal, red, and NIR bands, as well as metadata. This produces the scene’s LST. Table 1 summarizes the band numbers that are required for each satellite, where Landsat 7 uses bands 3, 4, and 6 while Landsat 8/9 uses bands 4, 5, and 10 for Red, NIR, and thermal bands, respectively. The Red and NIR bands are required to calculate NDVI (see 6), which is then used for the emissivity correction. The thermal band is used to determine the bright-ness temperature. The radiometric scaling parameters that is needed to transform raw Digital Number (DN) measurements into radiance, reflectance, and final land surface temperature are provided by the Metadata Text file (MTL) metadatam which accompanies satellite imagery, particularly Landsat data products. It contains essential metadata information that is needed for processing the raw data. DM is a raw pixel value stored by satellite sensors. They are uncalibrated digital values that represent the intensity of electromagnetic radiation that is recorded by the sensor for each spectral band. Since level-2 items already have processed surface temperature information, they are not utilized. The tool also includes an optional function that integrates a Large Language Model (LLM) to provide an interpretive summary of the output LST dataset. It collects basic descrip-tive statistics, including the minimum, maximum, mean, and standard deviation of LST values, along with raster metadata such as geographic reference, resolution, and scene name. This data is then submitted to the LLM via an API request. The generated summary provides a quick qualitative assessment of the scene’s thermal characteristics, including potential heat islands, natural temperature gradients, and anomalous thermal patterns. This outcome is documented in a text-based report together with the raster products. If the user chooses to disable the capability or fails to provide an API key, the report is gen-erated but contains only numerical data without interpretation. The LLM component enhances the usability of our system. It provides an automatically generated narrative that speeds up evaluation, decision-making, and the transmission of results to non-technical individuals. It does this without affecting the fundamental LST computation. IV. S YSTEM DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION 

We have used the ArcGIS Pro software to create the Land Surface Temperature (LST) tool by implementing a Python script through the ArcPy library. The tool contains 9 parameters, as shown in Figure 2. The first parameter is where the user adds input data files, and it can be a single Landsat scene or a parent folder containing multiple scenes. In the second parameter, the user can specify the output raster path. Additionally, the tool includes an optional thermal band selector that allows the users to select a specific thermal band as needed. This applies to Landsat 8 and 9: choose between band 10 and 11; otherwise, band 10 is automatically used. Followed by three parameters for optional saving of NDVI, emissivity, and brightness temperature rasters. The seventh parameter must be checked when adding batch scenes as input; otherwise, it is assumed that the input is a single scene. The last two parameters are for optional reporting using an LLM as follows: an API Key that must be obtained and provided to produce results. If left empty, a report file containing LST results statistics without interpretation will be generated.

V. R ESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

Our tool was tested on all three satellites and we have verified that it works as planned. For demonstration purposes, the results obtained from Landsat 8 are presented in this paper. The produced LST map is shown in Figure 3. The data indicate that temperatures range from approximately 25 to 50 degrees Celsius, which is a reasonable range for the Emirates of RAK in the UAE. Also, the LLM-based report is shown in Figure 4, which includes raster information, LST Statistics, and an interpretation of the results. To assess the tool’s efficiency, its running time is compared with manual processing time, assuming the manual processing time remains constant and is multiplied by the number of scenes.
