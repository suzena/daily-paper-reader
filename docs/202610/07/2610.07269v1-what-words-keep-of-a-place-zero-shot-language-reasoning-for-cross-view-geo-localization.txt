# What Words Keep of a Place: Zero-Shot Language Reasoning for Cross-View Geo-Localization 

Ayesh Abu Lehyeh 1, Jay Hwasung Jung 1, Safwan Wshah 1

# Abstract 

Cross-view geo-localization is commonly solved as an image retrieval problem, matching a ground-level image against a database of satellite tiles through a jointly trained embedding. Such models are accurate, but they need large paired su-pervision and cannot show what evidence supports a match. In this paper, we study a different question: how much of this task can be solved through language alone? We prompt a multimodal large language model (MLLM) to describe each ground panorama and each satellite tile as structured text, and localize by compar-ing these descriptions. No component is trained. We evaluate on 9,826 VIGOR pairs from four U.S. cities, in three settings. First, the descriptions are faithful but not discriminative. They agree closely across the two views, yet ranking the full pool by description similarity almost never returns the correct tile ( 0.39% 

Recall@1). Second, we narrow the pool to ten neighboring tiles, as a coarse prior would do. The same descriptions now become useful: an MLLM judge that scores structural consistency doubles random ranking and matches a strong lexical baseline. It also states which fields of the two descriptions agree and which conflict, which an embedding distance cannot do, and which we see as a step toward interpretable localization. Third, we place the judge on a trained visual retriever. On the queries it ranks wrongly, reranking from images works, while reranking from our descriptions does not ( 23 .5% against 10 .7% Recall@1). Scene structure survives the conversion into language, while the fine appearance detail needed to separate nearby places does not. Code and prompts are publicly available at https://github.com/AyeshAbuLehyeh/GeoLingual .

# 1 Introduction 

Cross-view geo-localization aims to find which satellite tile, out of a large georeferenced database, covers the location of a ground-level query image. The task matters whenever GPS is degraded [1, 2], for example in dense urban canyons, or for an embodied agent that tracks its position from what it sees [3]. Most current systems treat the problem as metric learning over image pairs [4–7], training an encoder so that a panorama and its satellite tile lie close together in a shared embedding space. This works well on benchmarks, but it has two drawbacks. First, accuracy depends on large amounts of paired supervision and tuned negative sampling. Second, the decision is opaque. An embedding distance does not show which parts of the scene produced the match, a limitation in the safety-critical settings that motivate the task. Recent work brings language into this pipeline, either by training text-to-satellite retrieval on MLLM-generated descriptions [8] or by using descriptions to rerank and explain visual candidates [9]. Our goal is different. Instead of training a language component and reporting the gain, we keep the pipeline zero-shot and study the description step itself. What does a structured verbal account of

a scene keep, and what does it lose? We prompt a multimodal large language model (MLLM) to convert both views into machine-readable XML descriptions of landmarks, road topology and spatial relations. The same model, queried text-only, then reasons over these descriptions to choose a tile. Nothing is fine-tuned: the descriptions come from prompting, the baselines get no contrastive training, and the judge has never seen a labeled cross-view pair. We use the word reasoning in a narrow sense. The judge does not compute a similarity score. It compares named fields across the two descriptions, states which agree and which conflict, and commits to a verdict that a reader can check against the text it was given. The decision is auditable, which an embedding distance is not. We therefore see this work as a step toward interpretable cross-view geo-localization rather than as a new retrieval method, and we do not claim that the rationale faithfully reflects the model’s internal computation (Section 5.1). The underlying question is about representation. A panorama and an overhead tile record the same place through very different sensors, and a description is a third record that depends on neither. Asking whether it supports localization is asking which properties of a scene survive being put into words. Our field ablation gives a partial answer: road topology and geometry carry the signal, orientation cues do not. The study proceeds in three steps. First, we confirm that the descriptions are consistent and semanti-cally aligned across the two views (Section 4.1). Second, we show that this alignment is not enough for retrieval: ranking every satellite description against a ground query gives a Recall@1 below 0.4% 

for all three text representations. The failure is one of distinctiveness, not accuracy, so we restrict the problem to verification over a small pool of geographically adjacent candidates, the situation a coarse prior would produce. Language reasoning becomes useful here, and states a rationale for each decision. Third, we place the MLLM on top of a pretrained retriever and rerank the queries it gets wrong. The outcome inverts. Reranking from images works, reranking from our descriptions does not improve on chance, and giving the model both modalities does not recover the difference. The limits here therefore come less from the reasoning step than from what the description step preserves. In this work, our contributions can be summarized as threefold: • We propose structured spatial verification, which recasts candidate ranking as a pointwise LLM-as-a-judge task over pools of geographically adjacent hard negatives (Section 4.2, Table 1b). It reaches 21 .0% Recall@1 against a 10% random baseline. This is ahead of dense embeddings and level with TF-IDF within sampling noise. It is also the only method here that explains itself: each decision names the fields that agree between the two views and the fields that conflict (Appendix D). A field ablation attributes most of the signal to road topology and geometry, while orientation cues alone fall to chance. • We present a retrieve-then-rerank study on the queries a trained retriever gets wrong. Image-based listwise reranking recovers 23 .52% of these failures, while text-based ( 10 .66% ) and fused image-plus-text reranking ( 11 .00% ) stay close to chance (Section 4.3, Figure 2). This indicates that concatenating the two modalities in a prompt dilutes the stronger signal instead of complementing it. • We give an account of when language helps in this task and when it does not, separating candidate sets that are geographically hard from those that are visually hard (Section 5). Two directions follow: training a verifier on our hard negative pools, and training the description model with a discriminability-aware objective. 

# 2 Related Works 

Cross-View Geo-Localization (CVGL). CVGL matches a ground-level image against a georefer-enced satellite database, and almost all methods solve it through learned visual similarity. Early work such as CVUSA [10], CVM-Net [11] and SAFA [12] compared ground and satellite features with CNN encoders and view-aware pooling. Later methods, including L2LTR [13], DSM [14] and GeoDTR [5, 6], improved accuracy with transformer-based matching and with geometric or domain alignment. The VIGOR dataset [4] made the setting more realistic. It dropped the assumption that a panorama sits at the center of its tile, and allowed one tile to cover several panoramas. This is the version of the problem we work in. In recent systems the largest gains come from the training objective rather than the backbone, combining contrastive losses with hard-negative sampling on modern convolutional encoders [7, 15, 16]. We use a model of this family as the retrieval front-end in 2Figure 1: Overview of the study. A prompted MLLM converts both views into structured XML descriptions. The same descriptions are then used in three settings: retrieval over the full pool, pointwise verification inside a small geographic neighborhood, and listwise reranking of the top-10 of a trained visual retriever. No component is fine-tuned. Setting 3 is scored on the 591 queries whose visual top-1 was wrong. Section 3.5. All of them depend on paired supervision and tuned negative mining, and none can be inspected after a match is made. 

Language-based cross-view localization. A recent line of work localizes from natural-language scene descriptions rather than from image features alone, and it is the closest to ours. CVG-Text pairs street views with MLLM-generated descriptions across three cities and trains a text-to-reference retrieval model, CrossText2Loc, reporting gains in accuracy and explainability [8]. GeoVLM uses vision-language descriptions to rerank cross-view candidates and justify the choice [9], and GeoX-Bench evaluates large multimodal models on cross-view correspondence at scale [17]. These papers ask how well a language-based pipeline can perform, and answer by training one. We ask a complementary question. With nothing trained, what does the description step keep and what does it discard? Our reranking experiment is informative here, since it identifies a regime in which description-based reranking carries no signal at all: the queries a trained retriever ranks incorrectly. 

Multimodal large language models. Models such as CLIP [18], BLIP-2 [19], LLaVA [20] and Qwen3 [21] make this approach possible. They name relations such as "a road curving left behind a row of trees" that conventional backbones encode only implicitly, and they do so in readable text. 

LLM-as-a-judge. A separate line of work uses language models to evaluate or rank content in place of human judgment or embedding similarity [22]. In information retrieval, rerankers such as RankGPT [23] prompt a model to order candidates against a query, and outperform embedding similarity when compositional judgments matter more than topical overlap. Related work asks a model to audit its own generations for unsupported content [24], and we adapt this as a grounding check in Section 3.4. We apply the judging paradigm to CVGL. Instead of ranking candidate satellite descriptions by cosine similarity, we prompt the same MLLM, text-only, to assess structural and spatial consistency between a ground query and each candidate. The usual application of LLM-as-a-judge targets open-ended response quality, and it is known to suffer from position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases. Our task is narrower, since the properties at issue are checkable in principle: road topology, orientation and landmark correspondence. 

# 3 Methodology 

3.1 Dataset 

We use VIGOR [4], which pairs 360 ◦ ground-level panoramas from Google Street View with satellite tiles covering the same area, together with GPS metadata. It contains roughly 90,000 panoramas and 10,000 satellite regions across four U.S. cities, and we work with 2,500 paired samples per city to limit inference cost (Appendix B). 3This subsampling affects how our numbers should be read. We take the first 2,500 rows per city of the official same-area test split, and neighboring panoramas can share a tile, so the reference pool holds 2,361 to 2,401 unique tiles per city, or 9,368 in total. That is roughly ten times smaller than the full VIGOR same-area pool. Absolute Recall@ K values here are therefore not comparable to figures reported on the full benchmark. Every method we evaluate uses the identical reduced pool, so comparisons within the paper remain valid, and Section 4.3 quantifies the effect. 

3.2 Textual Description Generation and Prompt Design 

No component is trained, so the prompts carry the work that training would otherwise do. Generic captioning was not sufficient in preliminary trials, since it produced text that could not separate one street corner from the next. We therefore wrote one prompt per modality. The satellite prompt enforces an allocentric, map-based perspective, and extracts cardinal orientations, roof fingerprints and linear sequences of static objects. The ground prompt enforces an egocentric perspective, and focuses on road topology, landmark visibility front and rear, and distinctive anchors. Both prompts constrain the output to a strict XML schema, which keeps the descriptions machine-readable by grouping cues into fixed categories. The schema was hand-designed and kept fixed for all experiments, so our findings describe this particular representation rather than language in general. Appendix A gives the full prompts and schemas. We use Qwen 3 VL 30B A3B Instruct [21] on NVIDIA H200 GPUs. The same model performs description generation, verification and reranking, always by prompting alone with greedy decoding; Section 5.1 discusses the resulting judge-generator overlap. 

3.3 Natural Language Processing and Analysis Framework XML parsing and structural repair. Generative models occasionally emit malformed markup. We therefore implemented a rule-based parser that validates every description against its schema, nor-malizing tag casing, closing unclosed tags, discarding overlapping blocks and stripping commentary outside the root element. Keeping only the samples that pass validation after repair recovers 9,826 valid pairs from the 10,000 generated samples, or 98 .3% , balanced across cities. The remaining 1.7% 

are excluded (Appendix E). 

Lexical distribution. We ran a Bag-of-Words analysis on the parsed content to check whether the two views describe the same physical entities. We lemmatize with spaCy , keep only nouns and proper nouns, and preserve domain bigrams such as "parking lot" as single tokens. We then compare the frequency of semantic anchors (e.g., tree , intersection , lane ) across modalities. 

Semantic embedding and zero-shot retrieval. We encoded the cleaned descriptions with a pre-trained BERT [25] model, mean-pooling the final layer, and scored each pair (Gi, S i) by cosine similarity, Sim (Gi, S i) = vGi · vSi /(∥vGi ∥∥ vSi ∥). For every ground description we rank all can-didate satellite descriptions in the reduced pool and report Recall@ K. Raw BERT embeddings are poorly suited to sentence similarity, so we treat them as a reference point and add two fur-ther representations on the identical pool: a sentence-transformer trained for semantic similarity (all-mpnet-base-v2 ) [26, 27], and a sparse TF-IDF vectorizer over the same lemmatized tokens. None of the three receives contrastive fine-tuning, and none is a modern retrieval-tuned encoder. We return to this scope limit in Section 5.1. 

3.4 Structured Spatial Verification as an LLM-as-a-Judge Task 

The retrieval formulation asks a representation to separate the true tile from thousands of candidates at once. Deployed systems rarely face that problem in its raw form, since a coarse prior from GPS, dead reckoning or a first-stage retriever narrows the search to a small neighborhood. We therefore reframe candidate ranking as a pointwise LLM-as-a-judge task inside such a neighborhood, as a verification step that follows a geographic prior rather than a replacement for retrieval. For a ground query and each candidate satellite description, the same MLLM is queried text-only and without images. It scores spatial consistency on a 0-10 scale and issues a MATCH or NO_MATCH verdict with a short rationale. The judge is conditioned on the structured fields already extracted for both views: road topology, spatial layout, orientation cues and distinctive anchors for the ground view, and cardinal orientation, road geometry, linear sequence and distinctive anchors for the satellite view. Candidates are ranked by this score. 4The candidate pools are deliberately difficult. Each contains the true tile and the nine geographically nearest wrong tiles in the same city, the nearest lying 55 to 61 meters away on average. These are the cases a coarse prior cannot resolve. We evaluate on 600 queries, and the three baselines of Section 3.3 score the identical pools, so any difference reflects the ranking mechanism rather than the candidate set. The baselines read the full parsed description, while the judge sees only the fields listed above, so the judge is not favored by having more input. Random ranking gives 10% Recall@1 and 50% Recall@5, our reference point throughout. Two auxiliary experiments probe this setting. A 

field ablation repeats the task on 120 queries per condition while exposing only part of the schema. A grounding audit re-presents 356 distinctive_anchors claims from 80 panoramas with their source images, labeling each GROUNDED, PARTIAL or HALLUCINATED. Appendix C details both. 

3.5 Vision-Retrieval-Augmented Reranking 

The verification setting assumes some external prior has produced the candidate pool. In our final experiment that prior is a real visual system, and we ask whether language reasoning adds anything on top of it. We use a pretrained cross-view retrieval checkpoint from AuxGeo [16], a ConvNeXt-base encoder trained contrastively with hard-negative mining on the VIGOR same-area split in the manner of Sample4Geo [7], and run it over our reduced pool to obtain, for all 9,826 queries, the top-10 candidate tiles and the rank of the true match. Because the retriever is close to ceiling here, reranking can only matter where it fails. We define the hard subset as the queries whose visual top-1 is wrong, and report recovery rate , the fraction of those queries whose true tile the reranker places first. The reranker is listwise rather than pointwise. All ten candidates appear in a single labeled prompt, and the model outputs a full ranking instead of an independent score per candidate. This follows standard practice in the LLM reranking literature [23], where comparing candidates outperforms judging each in isolation, and it suits near-duplicates whose differences are visible only by comparison. Holding the queries, pools and prompt structure fixed, we compare three reranker inputs: text only ,the structured descriptions of the query and all ten candidates; image only , the raw panorama and the ten raw tiles; and image and text , both concatenated in the same prompt. The variants run on identical query lists with the same seed, which makes their comparison a clean ablation of input modality. 

# 4 Results 

4.1 Lexical Distribution and Semantic Alignment 

The two modalities are consistent in what they mention. For every anchor we track, the frequency is close between the ground and satellite views within a city, so the prompts extract comparable content across the viewpoint gap. The absolute level is more revealing. The dominant anchors are near saturation, and "building" appears in more than 90% of descriptions in both views (Appendix B). A term occurring in nearly every description carries little discriminative value, so consistency and low discriminability are two readings of one measurement. The embedding analysis points in the same direction. Paired descriptions reach a mean BERT cosine similarity of 0.8748 , tightly concentrated around that mean (Figure 4 in the appendix). High similarity on true pairs is useful only if wrong pairs score lower, and this is where it breaks down: off-diagonal entries frequently exceed the diagonal, and mismatched pairs fall in the same narrow band as correct ones.

Explicit reasoning over structured fields improves on dense embedding similarity, but not on a well-built lexical baseline, because exact term overlap remains effective even against geographically close negatives. What the judge adds at comparable accuracy is an explanation. Every decision comes with a short rationale naming which structured fields agree across the two views and which conflict, for example a shared T-intersection against a roof layout that does not match. The baselines cannot do this: a retriever returns a distance and TF-IDF a set of overlapping tokens, so neither shows what the match rests on. An operator can check the rationale against the two descriptions and reject a match when the evidence is weak. Appendix D reproduces three decisions in full, and in the false accept the rationale itself exposes the error, since it claims an agreement that appears in only one description. Matching the strongest baseline while explaining itself is the claim we defend, and a better outcome than panel (a) suggests is possible from text. The field ablation (Appendix C) shows where this signal comes from. Exposing only road topology and geometry matches the full schema to within sampling noise, at 17 .5% against 20 .8% Recall@1 at n = 120 with an interval of ±7 points. Orientation cues alone fall to 5.8% , at or below the random baseline. Road geometry survives the change of viewpoint, while orientation cues are a coarse compass reading shared by most tiles in a city grid. Notably, the landmark fields carry no more signal than topology. Street names appear in those fields when the model recognizes a place, so this limits how much of the result could rest on memorized geography (Appendix D). A grounding self-audit over 356 anchor claims labels 92 .1% verifiable and 4.8% hallucinated. The auditing model also wrote the claims, so the first figure is optimistic and the second a lower bound (Section 5.1). 

4.3 Vision Retrieval and MLLM Reranking 

The pretrained retriever reaches 93 .99% Recall@1 and 99 .17% Recall@10 on our pool ( n = 9 ,826 ). Its reported Recall@1 on the full VIGOR same-area test set is 80 .43% , so the higher figure follows from a pool ten times smaller. Only 591 queries, about 6% , have an incorrect visual top-1 to repair, and we evaluate all three variants on this subset. If reranking never disturbed an already-correct top-1, image-only reranking would raise end-to-end Recall@1 from 93 .99% to 95 .40% , text-only to 94 .63% and the combined variant to 94 .65% , with a ceiling of 99 .16% . We report these as upper bounds, since we have not measured how often reranking damages a correct top-1. Two observations follow. First, text-only reranking is close to chance here, even though the same descriptions supported clearly above-chance verification in Table 1. What differs is the kind of difficulty: the candidates are geographically close in one case and visually confusable in the other. The tiles a contrastively trained model cannot separate are near-duplicates, and their descriptions collapse onto the generic vocabulary of Section 4.1. Second, presenting both inputs together does not simply fail to help: it returns Recall@1 to the text-only level while keeping part of the image advantage lower in the ranking. The likely reason is that near-identical generic text competes for attention with the sharper visual evidence. In the cases we inspected, the true tile and the selected distractor were described with the same intersection type and roof colors. We tested one fusion strategy, so this argues against naive concatenation and not against multimodal fusion in general. Together with Table 1, these results show where language reasoning helps: when candidates are geometrically distinct but lexically confusable, and not when they are visually near-identical. 

# 5 Discussion 

The three experiments point to a limit of the representation rather than of the reasoning applied to it. A description records what language names easily, such as road topology and coarse spatial relations, and omits what it names poorly, such as roof color, texture and precise footprints. Verification over geographically adjacent candidates succeeds because those candidates usually differ in what the text keeps. Reranking the retriever’s mistakes fails because they differ mainly in what it omits. There is also a practical asymmetry between the two representations. A retriever returns a distance, and nothing about that number can be argued with. Our judge returns an argument that can be checked against the two descriptions, so a wrong match can be traced to the field that misled it. This holds even when the two are equally accurate. Language is therefore better used as a channel for structure and explanation than as the measurement the decision rests on. All of this holds in the zero-shot setting we chose. We measure the content of prompted descriptions, not an upper bound on what a trained system could extract. 

5.1 Limitations 

• Generation and reasoning are not fully separated. Our evidence that the description step is the binding constraint is indirect. Descriptions that support verification fail on visually confusable candidates, while images on those candidates do not. The prompt format also changes between the two settings, from pointwise to listwise, so we cannot attribute the 7whole gap to description content. An oracle-description test, giving the judge descriptions known to contain a distinguishing cue, would separate the two effects and is the next experiment we plan. • Scope of the representation. The schema is hand-designed, so our claims concern this representation and this model rather than language in general. Panel (a) of Table 1 likewise bounds zero-shot matching over our descriptions, not text-based localization as such, and retrieval-tuned encoders would likely do better. • Self-audit and world knowledge. Both the verifier and the grounding audit reuse the model that wrote the descriptions. For verification this is milder, since the judge compares two descriptions produced in independent calls. In the audit the model assesses its own output, the self-preference case described in the judging literature [22], so 4.8% hallucinated claims should be read as a lower bound. Appendix D shows two forms of the problem: a rationale citing an agreement present in neither description, and a verdict resting on recognized street names. The rationales are inspectable, but not guaranteed faithful. • Partial overlap between the views. VIGOR panoramas are not centered on their tiles, so two faithful descriptions of one location can disagree. Our judge compares them as if they described the same content, which produces avoidable false rejections (Appendix D). • Evaluation scope. The reduced pool inflates absolute recall for every method, and single runs at n = 600 and n = 120 leave intervals of roughly ±3 and ±7 points, which is why we treat close orderings as ties. Reranking is reported on the hard subset, with end-to-end figures given only as upper bounds. 

5.2 Future Work 

Our results point to two separate bottlenecks, and both are worth naming. 

Contrastive alignment and a trained verifier. The retriever we use owes its accuracy to contrastive training with hard-negative mining, so the gap to a zero-shot judge is plausibly a training-signal gap rather than a capability gap. A natural next step is a projection trained to pull matching description embeddings together and push geographically near wrong tiles apart, using the pools from Section 3.4 as labeled data. 

Discriminability-aware description generation. Training the judge would not remove the second bottleneck. Text-only reranking landing close to chance suggests that the information needed to separate near-duplicate tiles is absent from the descriptions, not present but poorly used. No prompting strategy can recover what was never written down. Addressing this means training the description model with an objective that rewards text making the correct tile identifiable rather than merely accurate, which is closer to referring-expression generation than to caption fine-tuning. Given our fusion result, neither direction should concatenate modalities; an explicit fusion design and an image-only ablation would be needed. 

# 6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we studied what a structured linguistic description keeps and loses for cross-view geo-localization, using pretrained models zero-shot with prompts as the only degree of freedom. MLLM-generated descriptions are consistent and faithful across the viewpoint gap, but this does not make them distinctive, and ranking a large pool by description similarity almost never returns the correct tile. Once a coarse geographic prior narrows the pool, the same descriptions support verification about as accurate as a strong lexical baseline that, unlike any retrieval score, explains each decision in terms a person can check. This does not extend to candidates chosen for visual confusability. On the queries a trained retriever ranks wrongly, a reranker reading our descriptions performs close to chance, while one reading the images recovers a substantial share. We therefore see the verification stage as a step toward interpretable, reasoning-based cross-view geo-localization, and not as a replacement for visual retrieval. Two cautions follow. Concatenating a weaker language channel with a stronger visual one cost us most of the advantage of the stronger one, and a rationale that can be read is not always one that can be trusted. 8Acknowledgments and Disclosure of Funding

Two failures are visible here, and they are of different kinds. 18 Verifier output, score 2/10 , verdict NO_MATCH. "The ground-level description shows a cul-de-sac with a dead-end and a school building, while the satellite view shows a straight road with a central median and houses, indicating incompatible road topology and layout." 

This failure is the mirror image of the previous one, and it is not a reasoning error. Given the two descriptions, the verdict follows: a dead-end parking lot beside a school and a straight road with a central median and a row of houses are genuinely incompatible layouts. The premise is what fails. Because VIGOR panoramas are not centered on their tiles (Figure 3), the camera here stands in a parking lot near the edge of its tile, and the two descriptions faithfully report overlapping but largely different ground: the panorama is dominated by the lot, the running track, and the school, while the tile is dominated by the road and housing to one side. Both descriptions do share the track and the adjacent parking lot, but that agreement is buried among the disagreements produced by the offset. Description-based verification has no mechanism for partial overlap, since it compares two accounts as though they should describe the same content, which points to a concrete fix outside this paper: conditioning the judge on the expected offset, or asking for agreement over the overlapping sector rather than over the scene as a whole. 

# E Implementation Details 

The 9,826 schema-valid pairs recovered by the repair module are distributed evenly across the four cities: Chicago 2,469, New York 2,464, San Francisco 2,450, and Seattle 2,443. All description generation and all LLM verification and reranking calls use Qwen 3 VL 30B A3B Instruct with greedy decoding, served on NVIDIA H200 GPUs. Descriptions were generated once per image and cached; every downstream experiment reads from that cache, so the text-based methods are compared on byte-identical inputs. The verification experiment issues one LLM call per candidate, giving 6,000 calls for the n = 600 

pointwise run and 1,200 per ablation condition. The reranking experiment issues one call per query, presenting all ten candidates in a single prompt; we verified that the model’s chat template accepts multiple labeled images in one turn, with each image receiving its own vision block. Reranking runs at roughly 30 to 55 seconds per query depending on the input variant and the node, and the three variants were run as separate jobs over identical query lists with the same random seed. For the visual front-end we use a released AuxGeo-family ConvNeXt-base checkpoint trained on the VIGOR same-area split, without any further training or adaptation on our part, and we compute embeddings for the panoramas and for every tile in our reduced pool before taking the top-10 by cosine similarity. Before the main run we sanity-checked the checkpoint on a handful of hand-picked pairs, obtaining true-pair similarities of 0.54 to 0.62 against wrong-pair similarities of 0.13 to 0.20 ,and then validated it at scale against our pool as reported in Section 4.3. 19
